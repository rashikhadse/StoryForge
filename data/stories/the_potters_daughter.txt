THE POTTER'S DAUGHTER
(An original folk tale written for this repository. Public domain / CC0.)

In a river village where the road ended and the fields began, there lived an old potter named Ramdas and his daughter Meera. Ramdas had shaped pots on the same wheel for forty years, under the same neem tree, and the village said that water stayed cooler in his matkas than in anyone else's. But his eyes were failing, and in the dry year he had borrowed money from Seth Lalchand, the moneylender, to buy clay and wood.

Seth Lalchand came on the first day of the waning moon. He did not shout. He never shouted. He opened his ledger on the edge of the wheel and said, softly, that the loan must be repaid by the full moon, or the wheel and the courtyard would be his. Ramdas pressed his palms together and said nothing, because he had nothing to say.

That night Meera found the paper her father had signed with his thumb. "You cannot read, Bapu," she said. "How did you know what you were agreeing to?" Ramdas looked away. "Lalchand read it to me," he said. "He is a respectable man."

Meera did not sleep. She kneaded clay until her arms shook and fired pot after pot, meaning to sell them at the market. On the third night the rain came early and hard. It found the cracks in the kiln roof, and in the morning she lifted out nothing but broken, steaming shards.

So Meera did the only thing left. She washed the clay from her hands, put on her mother's old dupatta, and walked to Lalchand's shop by the market. "I would like to read the agreement myself," she said. Lalchand smiled at a girl asking to read, and handed it to her. Meera read it slowly, aloud, every word, while the brass scales gleamed in the dark. Near the end she stopped.

The clause was written in small, old-fashioned script, the kind used by the moneylender's grandfather. It said that if the debtor's wheel produced one thousand pots before the full moon, the debt would be considered paid in full. Lalchand had copied it from an old template and never noticed it. He noticed it now. His smile went thin. "No one wheel can make a thousand pots in twelve days," he said.

"One wheel cannot," said Meera. "But a wheel can turn for many hands."

She went from house to house. The weaver's sons came after their looms were quiet. The widow who sold sweets came with her grandchildren. Even the old priest came, and confessed that he had wanted to try the wheel since he was a boy. They worked at night so that Lalchand's spies would see nothing but a dark courtyard. Ramdas could not see the pots well anymore, but he could feel them, and he walked down the rows touching every rim, correcting the thin ones.

On the day of the full moon the panchayat met in the village square by the well. Lalchand insisted on counting every pot himself, tapping each one with his ring, hoping to find a crack. The whole village counted with him under their breath. Nine hundred and ninety-eight. Nine hundred and ninety-nine. One thousand.

Then a small boy ran up with one more pot, crooked and still damp, and set it down at the end of the row. "One thousand and one," said the headman, and he struck the debt from the record. Lalchand closed his ledger without a word.

That evening Ramdas sat at his wheel under the neem tree and set it spinning, and Meera began teaching the weaver's sons to read.
